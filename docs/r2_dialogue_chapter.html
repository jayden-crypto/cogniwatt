01

The question

A household robot is told “put the mug away.” Three mugs are in view. Before it can act it must decide which one is meant — and a misplaced object is not free to undo, so it needs to be confident, not merely better than chance.

The robot has four ways to resolve the ambiguity: think with a small on-device model, think with a larger one, offload to a model too big to fit on the device, or ask the person. Each costs energy, latency, or the human’s patience. Choosing between them is the reinforcement-learning problem the wider project studies. That problem only makes sense if the four channels are genuinely different, and this chapter measures how different they are.

Every number here replaces a placeholder. Nothing was estimated.

02

A dataset that cannot be gamed

The obvious dataset is degenerate. Ask a model to pick between three identical mugs and no model can beat chance, because there is no information in the text. Every scenario here instead turns on a commonsense inference that is inferable but not trivial:

“put the mug away” A. a red mug on the kitchen counter, half full of coffee ← correct B. a blue mug already sitting inside the cupboard C. a white mug hanging on its hook above the counter the others are already put away

720 scenarios were expanded from 60 hand-authored cores, of which 321 from 29 cores survive the audit in the next section — six nouns crossed with ten kinds of pragmatic inference. A core is one contrast: a target plus distractors that are each individually defensible as the wrong answer. Scenarios from one core are permutations of a single contrast and are therefore not independent, so every confidence interval in this chapter is a cluster bootstrap over cores, never over rows. The naive interval is 15–25% narrower and would be a false precision.

Three shortcuts were designed out and then verified: the correct answer’s position is exactly uniform, so a model that always answers first scores exactly 1/k; the correct answer’s colour is exactly uniform, and colours never appear in the authored descriptions at all; and the target is the longest description no more than three points above chance. The build fails rather than writes if any of these drift.

03

Three strangers checked the answers

A dataset whose ground truth is not defensible invalidates every number downstream, so the keys were audited rather than trusted. Sixty authored cores were shown to three annotators — Readers A, B and C, with Reader B from outside the project entirely — on a blind sheet: shuffled options, no answer key, no discussion. The gate was set before any of them started, and is deliberately brutal: a core survives only if every annotator independently picked the keyed answer.

Half the dataset did not survive. Reader C alone cost ten cores and 0.13 of κ — and pairwise agreement shows why: Readers A and B agree at 0.800, while Reader C agrees with either at about 0.63, reproduced across two separate sittings. One careful person resolves roughly a third of these differently from the other two. That is not carelessness; it is interpretive variance in the pragmatic families, and both figures are reported rather than the flattering one.

None of the 31 failed cores was re-keyed. Adjudicating them would mean choosing between readings three people had already split on, which is contestable for exactly the reason the core failed in the first place. Dropping needs no adjudication.

The obvious objection to this chapter is that the models look bad because the dataset is ambiguous. It is measurably not the explanation: on the cores the humans could not agree about, the models are no worse — the 3B is better, 0.632 against 0.500. The models fail on referring expressions that three independent people resolve identically.

The cost is real and worth stating plainly. Two families — proximity and exclusion — retain a single core each, and already_satisfied is down to nine items. No number in this chapter is broken out by those three families. The worked example in the previous section is drawn from already_satisfied because it is the clearest illustration of what a pragmatic inference is; it is not evidence of anything, and the family it belongs to no longer carries a measurement.

Neither had seen the ground-truth key before annotating, and Reader B, from outside, agreed with the key most often of the three (0.840), so the pool is not visibly biased toward the authored answers. Stated as a limitation rather than left to be discovered.

04

The instrument nearly broke the study

The measurement protocol specified numbering the options 0. 1. 2. and asking for “the number only.” Under that prompt the 0.5B model answered “1” on 237 of 240 prompts at temperature zero.

The tempting reading is that a tiny model cannot do pragmatics. The correct reading is that it was not choosing an option at all. Relabelling the options 1-based did not help — it still answered “1” just as often, now meaning the first option rather than the second. It has a prior on the token, not on the candidate.

What makes this dangerous is that the broken version looked healthy: a 99.8% parse rate, no errors, and a plausible number that would have entered the report as a finding about model capability. Every tier now sees A/B/C/D. This is not per-tier tuning — one template still serves all four — the template itself was wrong.

The 0.5B tier is barely above chance and falls apart as candidates are added. The 7B is the only one that stays comfortably clear of it.

The 7B’s accuracy is flat in k, and the smaller models’ is not

Going from two candidates to four costs the 0.5B twenty-one points and the 3B eighteen. It costs the 7B two. For the small models part of the difficulty is discrimination — more candidates, more chances to be pulled onto a distractor. For the 7B it is not: it either makes the pragmatic inference or it does not, and how many wrong options sit beside the right one barely matters. That is a qualitative change in failure mode, not just a quantitative one.

06

Asking twice does not help twice

Querying the same model again does not give a second independent opinion. A quantised model at low temperature is close to deterministic, and even when sampled its errors are systematically correlated — it misreads the same expression the same way. Information from a given model saturates.

This was measured as an intra-class correlation across ten repeats of each scenario, converted to effective independent draws, and fitted to the geometric decay the environment uses. Before any of it touched a real model, the estimator was validated against a generator whose correlation is known in closed form; it recovers a known value to within 0.03.

Ranked by γ alone, the 0.5B is the richest evidence source in the study and the 7B the poorest. That ordering is exactly backwards, and it is the most important methodological point in this chapter.

γ measures variability, not information. The 0.5B is close to random, and random answers look independent — its repeats are independent noise. The 7B is more self-consistent, so re-querying it genuinely buys less. Both facts are real, and neither can be read from γ without accuracy beside it. The project’s reward model composes them correctly; its prose must not quote γ on its own.

This also retires a diagnostic the protocol proposed: “γ ≈ 1 everywhere means the sampling temperature is too high.” Here the near-1 γ belongs to the weakest tier at an ordinary temperature, and lowering the temperature made that tier worse, not better.

07

The ceiling

Accuracy and γ only matter through the decision they enable. Placing an object is irreversible, so the robot should not commit below roughly a 0.85 posterior on the right referent. The question is therefore not how accurate a tier is, but whether querying it repeatedly can ever get there.

Running each tier through the environment’s own belief filter — imported and driven, not reimplemented, so it cannot drift from what training does — gives the confidence each channel converges to after unlimited queries.

No model available to a domestic robot — 0.5B, 3B or 7B — can ground these referring expressions confidently enough to act on, at any candidate count, with unlimited queries. Only asking the person can. Dialogue is not a fallback; it is the primary channel.

Two checks before believing it

The task is not impossible. The human channel clears the threshold comfortably at every candidate count, so the environment is solvable and the models simply cannot solve it. Had the human channel failed too, the honest conclusion would have been that the dataset is broken.

It does not balance on the 0.85 figure. Each channel is usable only if the threshold sits at or below its ceiling — 0.904 for the human, 0.731 for the 7B, 0.509 for the 3B, 0.300 for the 0.5B. For any threshold between 0.731 and 0.904, a band seventeen points wide, the human is the only usable channel. The conclusion survives a wide range of assumptions about how cautious a robot should be before an irreversible act.

Scale buys state-tracking, not judgement

Scaling from 0.5B to 7B does not lift every kind of inference equally, and the split is not random.

Everything scale fixes turns on reading an object’s current state or position: which was just used, which is already away, which is in use, which is broken, which is nearest. Everything scale barely touches turns on what a thing is for, who owns it, or what a destination can accept — normative and functional judgements rather than observational ones.

Model scale buys state-tracking. It does not buy normative reasoning.

This is also why the ceiling stalls. The largest tier’s residual errors are concentrated in exactly the families that scale does not fix — the four where even the 7B stays under 0.75 are purpose fit, affordance, exclusion and ownership — so a larger model would not obviously rescue it.

“could you put the bottle on the shelf” A. a blue bottle of oil that keeps at room temperature ← correct B. a red bottle of milk already sweating in the warm ← 7B chose only the shelf-stable one can go on a shelf

The model appears to read “sweating in the warm” as this one needs dealing with urgently rather than this one must not go on a shelf. It finds a salient cue and acts on it without checking it against what the destination can accept. Asked to put a mug in a drawer, it likewise chose a tall flared mug over one whose description states outright that it clears the drawer lip.

09

They were not being rushed

Every number so far comes from a prompt that asks for a bare letter — about five output tokens. That measures what these models do when told to answer immediately, which invites the obvious objection: they were never allowed to think. Since the 7B’s failures are pragmatic rather than perceptual, reasoning is exactly the kind of help that might have worked.

Same scenarios, same permutations, one new instruction: think step by step, then give the answer as a single letter on the last line.

Reasoning significantly harms the 3B and does nothing reliable for the 7B — at 70–90× the latency. A small model given room to think talks itself out of answers it would otherwise have got right.

The 3B spent 184 output tokens per answer against roughly five, and 2.9 seconds against 34 milliseconds. The 7B spent 10.4 seconds against 152 milliseconds. Nothing hit the 400-token cap, so no answer was truncated away before the model wrote it.

The 7B supplied its own control

In 34% of cases the 7B ignored the instruction and simply answered, in under twenty tokens. That splits the condition in two under one prompt: accuracy when it skipped reasoning, 0.811; accuracy when it actually reasoned, 0.801. Identical within noise. The gain is absent even where the model chose to reason — a stronger statement than the aggregate, and one no separate experiment would have produced.

Nor is it a near miss. Feeding the reasoning accuracy back through the belief filter gives a ceiling of 0.782; the best per-k figure reaches 0.813. To clear the threshold the 7B would need about 0.88 single-query accuracy. It is eight points short, and thinking closed a quarter of one point.

So the finding hardens: no model available to a domestic robot can ground these expressions confidently enough to act — and not because it was not allowed to think.

10

It is scale, not quantisation

A 3B model at four bits scoring 0.603 supports two very different claims: that 3B-class models are insufficient, or that aggressive quantisation destroys grounding. The second would be the more useful finding, because it would mean the tier is recoverable by shipping better weights. It was tested directly, with a near-lossless Q8_0 build of the same model on the same permutations.

Because both ran on identical permutations this is a paired comparison, which is far stronger than noting that the intervals overlap. The paired difference is −0.011 with a clustered 95% interval of −0.042 to +0.020, spanning zero; McNemar’s exact test gives p = 0.500; and the two builds agree on 581 of 660 scenarios. They do not merely score alike — they make the same mistakes.

So the limitation belongs to the model, not to numerical precision. It also closes the obvious objection to the ceiling result: the 3B’s ceiling is not an artefact of an aggressive edge quant, and better weights would not recover the tier. One practical note for deployment — the 2.0 GB build is at least as accurate as the 3.4 GB one, which on a 4 GB card is the difference between fitting and spilling, for no measurable accuracy cost.

The channel that works

Asking the person is only as good as the speech recognition behind it. Short answers to clarification questions were synthesised, mixed with noise at controlled signal-to-noise ratios, and transcribed.

Degrading the human channel by word error rate, as the environment originally did, understates it badly in exactly the noisy conditions that matter.

13

What would change these conclusions

  • The speech corpus is synthetic Utterances were produced by text-to-speech, which is cleaner and better articulated than human speech. The 0.034 word error rate is a lower bound and the human channel’s 0.937 effective accuracy an upper bound. Reaching the point where the human stops being the best channel needs a word error rate of 0.124 — 3.7× the measured value, which is an ordinary penalty moving from clean synthesis to real conversational speech.
  • The ground truth is not yet human-audited The 58 cores were authored by a model and verified structurally, not by independent readers. A blind audit sheet exists and needs two annotators. Every accuracy figure in this chapter inherits from that ground truth; if a core is mis-keyed, the numbers move.
  • One family is under-powered Sixty scenarios were dropped after measurement revealed a genuine ambiguity: when the instruction names the destination and a distractor already sits there, “the mug on the shelf” reads as an identifying description as easily as a destination. Dropping them widened the accuracy gradient, but it reduced the “already satisfied” family to twelve scenarios, so per-family claims about it are weak.
  • The offload tier is a stand-in It is a locally hosted 7B representing a model too large to sit on the device. This keeps every tier on one inference stack, so differences are model scale rather than serving infrastructure — but it is not a commercial frontier model, and a substantially stronger one might clear the threshold where this one does not.
  • The threshold is a modelling choice 0.85 comes from the reward structure of the surrounding environment, not from measurement. The conclusion holds anywhere in 0.731–0.904; outside that band it changes.
